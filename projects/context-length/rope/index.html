Deep Learning Context-Length Series · Part 2

Rotary position embeddings, one rotation at a time

Part 1 handed every token a position by adding a bank of sine waves onto it. Rotary embeddings throw that recipe out and do something stranger and simpler: they spin each token's query and key vectors by an angle set by its position. Because a dot product only cares about the angle between two vectors, the attention score falls out depending only on how far apart the two tokens are. This is the trick inside almost every modern long-context model, built here from the ground up.

The wish

What we actually wanted from a position

Part 1 ended on a small disappointment, and rotary embeddings are the fix. It is worth naming the disappointment first.

The sinusoidal scheme built a vector out of sine and cosine waves and added it onto each token's embedding at the very bottom of the model. That gave a nice property: the dot product between two positional vectors depended only on the gap between the positions, not on their absolute location. But that property lived on the positional part alone. The moment it was added to the word content the two were tangled together, and the whole signal was injected just once, at the input, then left to survive dozens of layers of mixing.

What we really want is sharper. Attention compares a query from one token with a key from another using a dot product, and that dot product is the attention score. The dream is for that score to depend on the two tokens' contents and on how far apart they sit, and on nothing else about where they are in the text. Not approximately. Exactly, and everywhere attention runs.

The target

Make the attention score between a token at position m and a token at position n depend only on their contents and on the gap n - m. Absolute position should leave no other trace.

The move

The whole idea: rotate, do not add

Here is the entire trick in one line. The rest of the page is just unpacking it.

Instead of adding anything to a token, rotate its query and key vectors by an angle proportional to the token's position. A token at position m has its query and key spun by an angle $m\theta$; a token at position n has its own spun by $n\theta$. Then let attention take its usual dot product, unchanged.

Why would that possibly help? Because of one fact about dot products that we will build up carefully in a moment: a dot product measures how aligned two vectors are, and rotating both of them by the same amount does not change how aligned they are. Only the difference in their rotations matters. Rotate the query by $m\theta$ and the key by $n\theta$, and the part of the score that comes from position depends only on $(n - m)\theta$, the gap. Absolute position cancels itself out. That is exactly the target we set, and it happens for free, with no extra vector added and nothing for the model to memorise.

Ground floor

Warm-up: what a dot product actually sees

No linear-algebra background needed here. A vector is just an arrow with a length and a direction, and the dot product between two arrows has one clean geometric meaning.

Take two arrows. Their dot product is their two lengths multiplied together, times the cosine of the angle between them:

$$ a \cdot b = |a|\,|b|\,\cos\varphi $$

where $\varphi$ is the angle between them. The lengths do not change when we spin the arrows around, so the interesting part is $\cos\varphi$: the dot product is largest when the arrows point the same way, zero when they are at right angles, and negative when they point apart. In one sentence, a dot product is a measure of alignment, and alignment is an angle.

Now the fact that makes rotary embeddings work. Suppose we rotate both arrows by the same angle. The angle between them has not moved, so their dot product is exactly what it was before. Rotating the two by different amounts does change the angle between them, by exactly the difference of the two rotations. Spin the arrows below and watch the readout: the dot product moves only when the two rotation angles differ.

That is the seed of the whole method. If we can make one arrow's rotation carry the query token's position and the other arrow's rotation carry the key token's position, their dot product will depend only on the difference between the two positions.

The link

Turning a position into an angle

Now we connect rotation to position. It is the shortest step in the whole piece.

Pick a single frequency, a fixed small angle $\theta$. For a token at position m, rotate its query arrow by $m\theta$: position 0 is not turned at all, position 1 is turned by $\theta$, position 2 by $2\theta$, and so on. Do the same to every token's key arrow using its own position. A token's position is now baked into the direction of its query and key.

When attention compares the query at position m with the key at position n, the warm-up tells us the score depends only on the difference of their rotations, which is $m\theta - n\theta = (m - n)\theta$. Slide the two positions below. Move them together, keeping the gap fixed, and the score holds perfectly still. Change the gap and it moves. Absolute position has become invisible to attention, exactly as we wanted.

Raise both sliders by the same amount so the gap stays fixed: the score freezes. That frozen score is the whole point of rotary embeddings.

In two dimensions, turning an arrow (x, y) by an angle $\phi$ is a small piece of standard trigonometry:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} $$

For rotary embeddings that angle $\phi$ is simply the token's position times the frequency, $m\theta$. Nothing more.

Scaling up

A ladder of rotation speeds

One rotation angle can only say so much, for the same reason a single sine wave could not in Part 1. The fix is the same idea: use many frequencies at once.

A real query or key vector has hundreds of dimensions, not two. RoPE cuts that vector into d/2 pairs of coordinates and treats each pair as its own little 2D arrow. Every pair is turned by the position, but each at its own frequency. The frequencies form a geometric ladder from fast to slow, the very same ladder as the sine waves in Part 1: pair i turns at frequency $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$.

So a token at position m spins its fast pairs through many turns and its slow pairs hardly at all. The fast pairs resolve fine, short-range differences in position; the slow pairs carry coarse, long-range position. Drag the position below and watch the row of wheels: the ones on the left race, the ones on the right creep.

Push the position far to the right: the fast wheels have spun through many whole turns while the slowest has barely moved. That spread of speeds is how one vector pins down both nearby and faraway positions at the same time.

Notice what rotation never does: it never changes an arrow's length. Every wheel's arrow stays pinned to its circle no matter the position. Hold onto that, because it is one of the reasons rotating beats adding.

The definition

The formula, in one picture

With the wheels in mind, the official formula is almost anticlimactic.

Stack all d/2 rotations into one big matrix that is zero everywhere except for the little 2x2 rotation blocks running down its diagonal, one block per pair. Call it $R_m$, the rotation for position m. RoPE replaces every query q and key k with $R_m q$ and $R_n k$ before attention takes their dot product.

$$ R_m = \begin{pmatrix} \cos m\theta_0 & -\sin m\theta_0 & 0 & 0 \\ \sin m\theta_0 & \cos m\theta_0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \cos m\theta_1 & -\sin m\theta_1 \\ 0 & 0 & \sin m\theta_1 & \cos m\theta_1 \end{pmatrix} \;\; \cdots \qquad \theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d} $$

(the blocks continue down the diagonal, one for each of the d/2 pairs). And the property that earns it all, which the warm-up already proved in two dimensions and which stacks cleanly across every pair:

$$ (R_m q) \cdot (R_n k) \;=\; q^{\top} R_{\,n-m}\, k $$

The right-hand side has only n - m in it. The absolute positions m and n have vanished, leaving the content vectors and the gap between the tokens. That one equation is the reason rotary embeddings exist.

The payoff

Why rotating beats adding

Rotary embeddings are not just a different flavour of Part 1. They fix three concrete problems at once.

  • Relative position is exact, not approximate. The gap n - m is the only positional quantity left in the score, by construction. There is no leftover absolute-position term that has to cancel out roughly, the way there was when we added sinusoids. Here it cancels exactly, in every attention dot product.
  • It acts on queries and keys, at every layer. Adding a signal at the input means the positional information has to survive being mixed and reshaped by every layer above it. RoPE turns the queries and keys right where attention uses them, so the relative-position property is renewed at each attention step instead of merely hoped for.
  • It never drowns the word. Recall the very first failed idea in Part 1, adding a big position number that swamped the embedding. Rotation cannot do that, because rotation never changes a vector's length. It only turns the vector, so the token's content is preserved intact and only its direction carries the position.

There is a fourth, quieter gift. Because far-apart tokens have their fast wheels turned into very different directions, those pairs contribute dot products that scatter around zero and tend to cancel, so RoPE hands attention a gentle built-in preference for nearby tokens that nobody had to design. It is a soft lean, not a hard cutoff, and like the ripples in Part 1 it is not perfectly clean, but it points the right way.

The context-length link

Stretching the window past what it trained on

This is where rotary embeddings meet the theme of the series: getting from a few thousand tokens to a few million.

During training a model only ever sees positions up to some length, call it L. That means each wheel only ever turns through the angles it can reach within L steps. Ask the trained model to read a sequence twice as long and the fast wheels swing into angles it has never seen, and it stumbles: the same failure to extrapolate we met at the end of Part 1.

The simplest repair is beautifully blunt. If the model is comfortable with positions up to L and we want to feed it 2L tokens, just pretend every position is half as large: turn by $(m/2)\theta_i$ instead of $m\theta_i$. Now position 2L lands on the same angle the model handled happily at L. This is position interpolation: squeeze more tokens into the same range of angles the model already trusts. The cost is that neighbouring tokens now sit closer together in angle, so fine positional detail gets a little blurrier, but the model keeps working far past its training length.

Slide the stretch factor below. At a factor of 1 the wheel turns at its normal rate and a long sequence spills past the range the model was trained on. Turn the factor up and every position is scaled down in lockstep, packing the whole sequence back inside the trusted arc.

At a big stretch factor the ticks for consecutive positions crowd together. That crowding is the price of interpolation: a loss of fine positional resolution in exchange for far more reach.

Position interpolation is the plain version. The methods behind today's long-context models refine it: NTK-aware scaling stretches the slow frequencies more than the fast ones, so short-range detail is kept while long-range reach grows, and YaRN tunes this frequency by frequency with a couple of extra tricks. They are all variations on the single move the knob above performs: change how fast the wheels turn so a fixed budget of trusted angles covers many more tokens. That is a large part of how a model born with a 512 or 4096 token window ends up reading a whole book.

Closing

The whole thing, in one breath

Attention cannot see order, so we have to hand it position. Part 1 added a signal; rotary embeddings turn the query and key instead.

Rotate each token's query and key by its position, using a ladder of frequencies. A dot product sees only the angle between two arrows, so absolute position cancels and the attention score depends only on the gap between tokens: exact relative position, refreshed at every layer, with the word's meaning left untouched because rotation never changes a length. Then, because position is now just an angle turned at a known rate, we can stretch the window simply by turning the wheels a little slower. That is rotary embeddings, and it is most of the reason a modern model can read far more than it was ever trained on.

Sinusoidal encoding writes position into what a token is. Rotary encoding writes it into which way the token points, and that small change is what carried context windows from a few thousand tokens toward a few million.
